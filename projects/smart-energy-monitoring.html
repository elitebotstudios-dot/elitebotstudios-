Smart Energy Monitoring.
A measurement chain, modelled, designed and compiled.

A low-voltage DC energy monitor built through three layers, each with its own evidence: a software model verified against reference cases, a native KiCad schematic that passes ERC, and firmware that cross-compiles for the ESP32. The prototype does not exist yet, and the page says so where it matters.

01 / ENGINEERING OBJECTIVE

How far is an energy reading from the truth?

A monitor that reports 12.00 W is making a claim about a chain of components. A divider scales the voltage, a shunt converts current into a small differential voltage, an amplifier and an ADC translate both into numbers, firmware multiplies them, and a clock decides how long to keep adding. Each stage contributes an error the others do not cancel, and some of those errors — a gain error, a stale sample, an interval integrated across missing data — look exactly like a real change in the load.

The useful engineering question is therefore not “what does it read?” but “how far can that reading be from the truth, which stage put it there, and how would anyone know?” This project answers that question three times over: once in a simulation where every stage can be varied independently, once as a schematic where the connections must be electrically legal, and once as firmware where the arithmetic has to survive real timestamps and missing samples.

In scope

Low-voltage DC (5–12 V, 0–0.5 A), a single non-isolated measurement channel, sampled power and integrated energy, and the evidence needed to check all of it.

Out of scope

Mains and AC, isolation, three-phase, battery management, wireless reporting. Any accuracy claim about physical hardware.

03 / SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

One circuit, described three ways.

The same signal path appears as a simulatable model, as a schematic, and as firmware. Keeping the three separate is what makes it possible to check one against another.

04 / SOFTWARE MODEL

Everything measurable, before anything is soldered.

The software model is the project's first deliverable and its reference implementation. It solves the source and the load together — a resistive load derives its own current from the applied voltage — then passes that operating point through a configurable sensing chain and accumulates energy with the trapezoidal rule over the actual interval between samples.

What it can establish

  • The arithmetic, exactly where the arithmetic is exact: 12 V at 1 A for an hour is 12 Wh, to the last digit.
  • How much a gain error, offset, noise or quantisation step moves the reading, and whether calibration recovers it.
  • What a step that falls between two samples costs, quantified: 0.0093 % of a 27 000 J run at 1 s sampling.
  • That missing samples cannot create energy: an invalid reading costs two intervals and is counted.

What it cannot establish

  • Anything about a physical sensor. The noise source is seeded and statistical, not a characterisation of a part.
  • ADC non-idealities: no INL, DNL or reference drift.
  • Loop dynamics, thermal behaviour, or what the load does faster than the sample interval.
  • That the model's sensing chain equals the INA226's. It is a model of a chain, not of that part.

05 / ELECTRICAL DESIGN

ESP32 and INA226, wired so the reading means something.

The measurement is a 0.1 Ω shunt in series with the load, with an INA226 reading the differential voltage across it and the bus voltage on the load side. That last detail is the reason this revision exists: on the bare INA226, VBUS is a separate pin, and if it is not tied to IN− the reported voltage comes from the wrong node and every power figure is wrong while looking perfectly plausible.

06 / EMBEDDED FIRMWARE

Decode, timestamp, integrate — and refuse to guess.

The firmware is split so that the arithmetic can be tested without a board. A platform-free core holds the register decode, the calibration, the energy accumulator and the missing-data policy; a thin application layer implements I²C with Wire and prints CSV over serial. The host tests drive the same core through a mock bus, so the code that compiles for the ESP32 is the code that was tested.

  1. 01
    Decode

    Signed 16-bit shunt register at 2.5 µV per bit, unsigned bus register at 1.25 mV. Current is derived from the shunt voltage and the known resistance rather than from the device's calibration register, so a mis-written register cannot silently corrupt the reading.

  2. 02
    Timestamp

    Milliseconds since boot in an unsigned 32-bit counter. Subtraction is wrap-safe, so the 49.7-day rollover is not a bug waiting to happen. There is no real-time clock in this design, so no date is claimed.

  3. 03
    Integrate

    Trapezoidal rule over the measured interval. An invalid or missing sample holds the interval instead of integrating a stale reading, and a gap longer than five seconds is not integrated at all. Both are counted, so a period with no measurement cannot look like a period of zero power.

  4. 04
    Report

    CSV on serial: timestamp, voltage, current, power, energy, status — with comment lines for intervals that were held.

08 / ENGINEERING DECISIONS

Choices, with what they cost.

Design decisions, the alternative and the cost
DecisionAlternative rejectedWhat it costs
INA226 over INA219INA219: 12-bit, ±320 mV, 26 V busFewer bits of headroom on current; the INA226 gives 16-bit conversion for the same wiring effort.
0.1 Ω shunt0.05 Ω for a 1 A rangeCaps the design at 0.819 A. A 1 A test needs a different shunt and a fresh calibration.
Reverse diode upstream of the shuntDiode after the shunt, or none0.3–0.4 V of headroom and ~0.2 W at 0.5 A, in exchange for a measurement the diode cannot corrupt.
Trapezoidal integrationSum of power samplesOne stored sample and two multiplications. The alternative is not an approximation: it is dimensionally wrong, and out by 2× at 0.5 s sampling.
Hold missing intervalsIntegrate across the gapLoses a little energy instead of inventing some. The loss is counted and reported.

Bare IC over a breakout moduleModule with an on-board shuntHarder to assemble (VSSOP-10), but the VBUS connection is explicit instead of vendor-dependent.
09 / BUILD AND TEST PLAN

From schematic to first reading.

The plan is deliberately slow, because every stage is cheaper to fail than the one after it. Each step has an observable exit condition, and the stop conditions are written down before anything is energised.

  1. 1
    Review the design

    Read the ERC report, the design review and the open questions. Decide bare IC or module.

  2. 2
    Identify the parts

    Confirm markings against datasheets; measure the shunt; check the module's pull-up rail and VBUS connection with a multimeter.

  3. 3
    Unpowered continuity

    Trace the current path; prove there is no short from the protected rail to ground.

  4. 4
    First power-up

    5 V, 100 mA limit, no load, second person present. Nothing warm, no sensor error on serial.

  5. 5
    Communication

    I²C scan finds 0x40; the identity registers read back correctly.

  6. 6
    Known loads

    100 Ω and 47 Ω at 12 V; record voltage, current, power and energy against a multimeter.

  7. 7
    Calibration

    Six points across the range against a reference meter, in a recorded environment, failures included.

10 / OUTSTANDING WORK

What this project does not have.

  • No prototype. No component has been purchased, assembled or energised. Everything above was verified without touching hardware.
  • No PCB. The schematic is a schematic. No layout, routing or design-rule check exists.
  • No flashed firmware. The binary compiles and links for the target; it has never been uploaded to a board.
  • No measurements. Not one number in this project came from a physical sensor.
  • No calibration. The procedure exists; the results do not.
  • No accuracy claim. Until a reference-meter comparison exists, the design has a stated target of ±1 % voltage and ±2 % current and no evidence for it.
